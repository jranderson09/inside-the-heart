Shadowing · 50+ hours

Shadowing Dr. Curtis Anderson, Cardiac Surgery, REX Hospital, Raleigh

Over 50 hours alongside Dr. Curtis Anderson at REX Hospital, I learned that surgery begins before the operating room. Dr. Anderson spoke in a calm, steady manner and walked the patient and family through each step of what he would do. His knowledge was unmistakable, and I watched the family relax. Inside the operating room, everything was focused and precise. After a successful surgery, I saw how grateful the family was. That moment showed me the kind of surgeon I want to become: one who can bring families that kind of relief.

Reading · Phlebology, 2024

Compression therapy in the management of varicose veins

One open question in my reading stayed with me: should people with early, symptom-free venous disease (classes C1 and C2) use compression to keep it from getting worse? Compression lowers pressure in the veins, but no well-designed studies have tested whether it slows progression, so doctors cannot yet make an evidence-based recommendation. The authors call for a large randomized trial comparing options head to head. It showed me that even common treatments have unanswered questions, and that answering them takes careful research.

Compression Therapy for Chronic Venous Disease: What the Evidence Says and Where It Falls Short

Jacob Anderson · A narrative literature review, not original research.

Abstract

Chronic venous disease (CVD), which includes varicose veins, is commonly managed with compression therapy. This review summarizes recent guidance and studies on how well compression works, how long patients should use it, and why some patients stop. Compression is widely recommended and helps with symptoms and complications, but evidence on the best daily duration is limited and adherence remains a challenge. I connect these findings to more than 300 hours of volunteering at a compression care center.

1. Background

CVD affects the veins of the legs, where weakened walls and valves let blood pool. It involves the veins rather than the heart itself, but it belongs to the wider field of vascular medicine that cardiovascular and vascular surgeons work within. Compression has been used to treat venous disease for centuries [1] and is widely prescribed with guideline support [2].

2. What the evidence shows

A 2024 guidance document from the International Union of Phlebology reports that compression reduces ulceration and is effective for symptoms of CVD, including swelling [3]. A 2008 meta-analysis of 11 randomized trials (1,453 participants) found that stockings with 10 to 20 mmHg of ankle pressure reduced swelling and symptoms, while lower pressure was ineffective and higher pressure added no clear benefit [4]. The authors noted uneven reporting and study methods, and the analysis was commissioned by an outside company that, they state, did not influence its design or interpretation [4]. A recent set of clinical guidelines made 30 recommendations across seven areas of venous disease. Seven were strong and 17 rested on high-quality evidence, which the authors say highlights the need for more research [5].

3. Gaps in the evidence

Much of the literature focuses on venous leg ulcers, on comparing compression with procedures, and on compression after procedures, rather than everyday use [3]. A 2023 systematic review of 41 studies found no established data on the best daily duration, and the studies were too different in design and quality to combine statistically [2]. The same 2024 guidance notes that how long compression lasts often depends on clinician preference [3]. It also identifies an open question: whether people with early, symptom-free disease (classes C1 and C2) should use compression to prevent progression. No well-designed studies have tested this, so no evidence-based recommendation can be made, and the authors call for a large randomized trial to settle it [3].

4. The adherence problem

A published case report describes an 88-year-old patient whose leg ulcer improved with compression, returned after two months of not using it, and improved again once she was given a structured program with demonstrations, written instructions, reminders, and weekly follow-ups [6]. A single case cannot prove a general rule, but it shows why patient education may matter as much as the stockings themselves.

5. Reflection from my volunteer work

Over four years and more than 300 hours as a volunteer, I took calls, ran packages to the post office, helped build the company's website, and worked directly with clients, many of them elderly, who came in with varicose veins and other circulatory concerns. Some clients were frustrated or difficult, and I learned that patience and close attention to each person's needs matter as much as the product itself. That experience shapes how I read the research above: if sticking with compression is a real challenge for patients, then clear communication and patient support are part of the treatment.

6. Conclusion and limitations

The sources agree that compression helps, but they leave open how long it should be worn and how to help patients keep using it. This is a short narrative review of a small set of sources, not a systematic one, and it is not medical advice. As someone hoping to become a cardiovascular surgeon, I see this as a reminder that good outcomes depend on what happens after treatment as well as the procedure itself.
